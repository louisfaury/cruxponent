For its own sake or as part of a policy iteration scheme, evaluating policies is an important matter. 
This post is concerned with off-policy policy evaluation, or evaluating policies with data generated by others.
The ambition is to provide a smooth progression towards the Retrace estimator and some of its extensions, with a focus on
each operator's properties and stochastic approximation variant.

## Setting and notations
We work with _finite_ MDPs $\mathcal{M}:=(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, p, r)$ for simplicity. 
All arguments extend to continuous settings.
Under a discounted objective, we will develop policy evaluation operators for state-action values $q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$ (exception made
of Vtrace {{< ref link="vtrace">}} [6]{{< /ref>}} which operates on state values).
For any policy $\pi$ and $(s, a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$:
$$
q\_\lambda^\pi(s, a) := \mathbb{E}\_{s,a}^\pi\big[\sum\_{t\geq 1}\lambda^{t-1}r(s\_t, a\_t)\big]\\;.
$$
We recall below some state-action values _vectorial_ notations, for completeness.
The state-action values $q$ and the reward $r$ are considered as vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$.
For any stationary policy $\pi$, the right-stochastic matrix
$\mathbf{P}\_\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A})\times(\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A})}$ collects the transition kernel coupled with $\pi$;
for any $(s,a,s^\prime,a^\prime) \in (\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^2$:
$$
[\mathbf{P}\_\pi]\_{(s,a),(s',a')} := p(s'\vert s,a)\pi(a'\vert s')\\;.
$$

## On-policy warm-up

### Bellman operator
Let $\pi$ some stationary policy. The Bellman evaluation operator writes, for $q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi : q &\mapsto \mathbf{r} + \lambda \mathbf{P}\_\pi q \\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
Analogously to the state-value case (see [this post](../mdp_basics_2)) $\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi$ defines a contractive operator (wrt the sup-norm) whose unique fixed point is $q\_\lambda^\pi$.
This guarantees that for any $q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$ the fixed-point algorithm $q_{k+1} = \mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q_k$
converges linearly fast to $q\_\lambda^\pi$—but applying this operator requires knowledge of the transition kernel $p$
and, for large state-action spaces, involves large matrix-vector products.

In practice, one resorts to stochastic approximations (à la Robbins-Monroe) of fixed point algorithms.
Concretely, given some experience $(s\_k, a\_k, s\_{k+1}, a\_{k+1})$ triggered by $\pi$ and some 
estimator $q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$, denote
$\delta\_k^\pi := r(s\_k, a\_k) + \lambda q(s\_{k+1}, a\_{k+1}) - q(s\_k, a\_k)$
its associated temporal difference error (also known as the Bellman residual).
Provided a step-size $\alpha>0$, the estimator $q$ can be updated according to the SARSA rule:
$$
\tag{1}
q(s\_k, a\_k) \leftarrow q(s\_k, a\_k) + \alpha\delta\_k^\pi\\;.
$$

### Multi-steps operators
The update rule exposed in (1) is essentially the SARSA algorithm.
It looks at single transitions to update the current estimate $q\_k$—we now extend it
to handle _sequences_ of experience.
For any $n\geq 1$, we override our notations to write
$\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda,n}^\pi := (\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi)^n$.
The operator $\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda,n}^\pi$ is multisteps and expands over several transitions:
$$
\tag{2}
\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, n}^\pi q = \sum\_{t=0}^{n-1} \lambda^t \mathbf{P}\_\pi^t \mathbf{r} + \lambda^{n}\mathbf{P}\_\pi^{n}q\\;.
$$
Observe that $q\_\lambda^\pi$ is still a fixed-point for this operator. Further, $\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda,n}^\pi$ contracts wrt. the sup-norm—which guarantees the uniqueness of the fixed point, and the healthiness of (sampled) fixed-point iterations.

{{% toggle_block background-color="#FAD7A0" title="Proof" default-display="none"%}}
For any $q, q^\prime\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$ we have:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\\|\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda,n}^\pi q - \mathcal{T}\_{\lambda,n}^\pi q^\prime\\|\_\infty &= \\|\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi\big((\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda}^\pi)^{n-1}(q-q^\prime)\big)\\|\_\infty\\;,\\\
&\leq  \lambda\\|(\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda}^\pi)^{n-1}(q-q^\prime)\\|\_\infty\\;, &(\mathcal{T}\_\lambda \text{ contracts})\\\
&\leq  \ldots &(\text{repeat})\\\
&\leq  \lambda^n\\|q-q^\prime\\|\_\infty\\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
<div style="text-align: right"> $\blacksquare$&nbsp; </div>
{{% /toggle_block %}}

For stochastic approximations, using larger values for $n$ reduces the _bias_ (wrt the true $q\_\lambda^\pi$)
introduced by bootstrapping over the current estimator $q$.
That comes with a price: updates that rely on longer trajectories have more _variance_.
In the limit where $n\to\infty$ we will recover the (unbiased) Monte-Carlo estimator for $q\_\lambda^\pi$.

### Exponential averaging
To avoid settling for a single value for $n$ when using updates like (2), one can average them—typically, according
to an exponential schedule.
Going back to the Bellman operator; for any $\gamma\in[0,1)$ define:
$$
\tag{3}
\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda,\gamma}^\pi := (1-\gamma)\sum\_{n\geq 1} \gamma^{n-1} \mathcal{T}\_{\lambda,n}^\pi\\;.
$$
This operator contracts wrt. sup-norm, and its unique fixed-point is still $q\_\lambda^\pi$.
A useful identity to analyse $\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda,\gamma}^\pi$ is given in (4) and holds for any $q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$.
It is direct from this expression that picking $\gamma=0$ falls back to the single-step operator $\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi$,
while $\gamma=1$ yields the Monte-Carlo estimator. 

$$
\tag{4}
\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^\pi q = q + (\mathbf{I}-\lambda\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\pi)^{-1}(\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q -q)\\;.
$$

{{% toggle_block background-color="#FAD7A0" title="Proof" default-display="none"%}}
Observe that for any $n\geq 0$ and $q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$ by definition of $\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, n}$ we have:
$$
\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, n+1}^\pi q - \mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, n}^\pi q = \lambda^n \mathbf{P}\_\pi^n (\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q -q )\\;.
$$
A telescopic sum therefore gives that for any $n\geq 0$:
$$
\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, n}^\pi q = q + \sum\_{t=1}^n \lambda^{t-1} \mathbf{P}\_\pi^{t-1} (\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q -q )\\;.
$$
Therefore for any $\gamma\in[0,1)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^\pi q &= (1-\gamma )\sum\_{k\geq 1}\gamma^{k-1}\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, k}^\pi q\\;, \\\
&= q + (1-\gamma )\sum\_{k\geq 1}\gamma^{k-1}\sum\_{t=1}^k \lambda^{t-1}\mathbf{P}\_\pi^{t-1}(\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q -q )\\;,\\\
&= q + (1-\gamma) \sum\_{t\geq 1} \lambda^{t-1}\mathbf{P}\_\pi^{t-1}\big(\sum\_{k\geq t} \gamma^{k-1}\big)(\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q -q ) \\;,\\\
&= q +  \sum\_{t\geq 1} \lambda^{t-1}\gamma^{t-1}\mathbf{P}\_\pi^{t-1}(\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q -q )\\;,\\\
&= q + (\mathbf{I}-\lambda\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\pi)^{-1}(\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q -q )\\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
<div style="text-align: right"> $\blacksquare$&nbsp; </div>
{{% /toggle_block %}}

The state-action indexed version of (4) will be useful later; it writes that for any $(s, a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$:
$$
\mathcal{T}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^\pi q(s, a) = q(s, a) + \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^\pi\big[\sum\_{t\geq 0}\lambda^t\gamma^t \big(r\_t + \lambda q(s\_{t+1}, a\_{t+1}) - q(s\_t, a\_t)\big)\big]\\;.
$$
The associated stochastic approximation algorithm writes
$
q(s\_k, a\_k) \leftarrow q(s\_k, a\_k) + \alpha\sum\_{t\geq k} (\lambda\gamma)^{t-k}\delta\_t^\pi\\;.
$
It assumes access to an infinite sequence—or at least one that reaches an absorbing state.
This implementation is known as the forward view of SARSA($\lambda$) and is hardly practical.
Much more appropriate is its backward view alternative, which uses _eligibility traces_ to update the estimator (not covered here).


{{% toggle_block background-color="#CBE4FE" title="Bias-variance tradeoff " default-display="none"%}}
Both multi-steps and exponential-averaging operators trade off bias for variance by, respectively,
letting $k\to\infty$ or $\gamma\to 1$.
This naturally impacts convergence speed, and one can therefore inquire about the 'sweet spot' for those parameters.
We refer the reader to {{< ref link="biasvariance">}} [2]{{< /ref>}} for a detailed 
discussion on the topic (albeit with slightly modified algorithms, for the sake of the analysis).
{{% /toggle_block %}}


## Importance Sampling
So far we assumed online access to experience generated by the policy $\pi$ we wish to evaluate.
Below, we consider the off-policy setting: experience is generated instead by a fixed behavioural policy $\mu$.
Observe that Monte-Carlo estimators can easily be provided in that off-policy context.
Indeed, under some regularity assumptions, trajectory-level importance sampling yields:

$$
q\_\lambda^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}\_{s,a}^\mu\Big[\sum\_{t\geq 1} \lambda^{t-1}r(s\_t, a\_t)\prod\_{j=2}^t\frac{\pi(a\_j\vert s\_j)}{\mu(a\_j\vert s\_j)} \Big]\\;,
$$
with the convention that an empty product resolves to the constant $1$ (at $t=1$).
{{% toggle_block background-color="#FAD7A0" title="Proof" default-display="none"%}}
The proof adopts a trajectory view. Let $t\geq 1$ and denote $\tau\_t = (s\_1, a\_1, \ldots, s\_t, a\_{t})$ 
a trajectory of length $t$ with $s\_1=s$, $a\_1=a$ fixed.
Denote $r\_\tau:= r(s\_t, a\_t)$ the last reward perceived along $\tau$. Then: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbb{E}\_{s,a}^\pi[r(s\_t, a\_t)] &= \sum\_{\tau} \mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\pi(\tau\_{t}=\tau)r\_\tau\\;, \\\
                                     &= \sum\_\tau \frac{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\pi(\tau\_t=\tau)}{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\mu(\tau\_t=\tau)}\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\mu(\tau\_t=\tau)r(s\_t, a\_t) \\;,\\\
                                     &=  \mathbb{E}\_{s,a}^\mu\Big[ r(s\_t, a\_t) \frac{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\pi(s\_{2:t}, a\_{2:t})}{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\mu(s\_{2:t}, a\_{2:t})}\Big]\\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
The ratio of trajectory probabilities simplifies, as they both involve the same transition kernel;
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\pi(s\_{2:t}, a\_{2:t})}{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\mu(s\_{2:t}, a\_{2:t})} &= \frac{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\pi(s\_{2:t}, a\_{2:t-1})}{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\mu(s\_{2:t}, a\_{2:t-1})}\frac{\pi(a\_t\vert s\_t)}{\mu(a\_t\vert s\_t)}\\;,\\\
    &= \frac{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\pi(s\_{2:t-1}, a\_{2:t-1})}{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\mu(s\_{2:t-1}, a\_{2:t-1})}\frac{p(s\_t\vert s\_{t-1}, a\_{t-1})}{p(s\_t\vert s\_{t-1}, a\_{t-1})}\frac{\pi(a\_t\vert s\_t)}{\mu(a\_t\vert s\_t)}\\;, \\\
    &= \frac{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\pi(s\_{2:t-1}, a\_{2:t-1})}{\mathbb{P}\_{s,a}^\mu(s\_{2:t-1}, a\_{2:t-1})}\frac{\pi(a\_t\vert s\_t)}{\mu(a\_t\vert s\_t)}\\;,\\\
    &\quad \vdots\\\
    &=\prod\_{j=2}^t \frac{\pi(a\_j\vert s\_j)}{\mu(a\_j\vert s\_j)}\\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
Applying this result directly in the definition of $q\_\lambda^\pi$ yields the announced claim.
<div style="text-align: right"> $\blacksquare$&nbsp; </div>
{{% /toggle_block %}}

This estimator is unbiased but its variance grows _exponentially_ fast wrt. the horizon.
The operators introduced in the next sections precisely aim at
taming this variance by bootstrapping and/or clipping the per-step importance ratios.
We will discuss several approaches; for each we will follow the same protocol: introduce an off-policy operator, show that $q\_\lambda^\pi$ 
is its
unique fixed-point (uniqueness being guaranteed by each operator's contractive nature), and derive its stochastic approximation before discussing some key properties.


## Off-policy $Q(\lambda)$  {{< ref link="qlambda">}} [3]{{< /ref>}}
Our starting point for the off-policy $Q(\lambda)$ operator is the on-policy exponential average formula of (4).
The idea for this operator is to 'ignore' that trajectories are sampled by $\mu$ and keep the Bellman residual of $\pi$
without any probabilistic correction. Concretely, for any $\gamma\in[0, 1)$ and $(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$:
$$
\tag{5}
\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{{\color{black}\pi}, {\color{black}\mu}} q(s, a) := q(s, a) + \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\color{black}\mu}\big[\sum\_{t\geq 0} \gamma^t\lambda^t \big(r\_t + \lambda\mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim{\color{black}\pi}(s\_{t+1})}[q(s\_{t+1}, a^\prime)] - q(s\_t, a\_t)\big)\big]\\;.
$$
The equivalent vectorial form (5) can be also compared to the on-policy case of (4); observe that 
the kernel $\mathbf{P}\_\pi$ is replaced by $\mathbf{P}\_\mu$—the one we can actually use for sampling experience
under the off-policy constraint:
$$
\tag{6}
\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q = q + (\mathbf{I}-\lambda\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\mu)^{-1}(\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q -q)\\;.
$$

It is direct from (6) that $q\_\lambda^\pi$ is a fixed-point of $\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}$. Contraction, however, is more delicate: it only holds when $\pi$ and $\mu$ are _close enough_—where 'enough' is captured by the discount factor $\lambda$.
This is not surprising given that no effort was made to 
correct the bias introduced by following $\mu$ (via, _e.g._, importance sampling).
Concretely, if $\pi$ and $\mu$ satisfy $\max\_{s}\\| \pi(s) - \mu(s) \\|\_1 < \frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda\gamma}$ then for any $q\_1, q\_2 \in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$:
$$
\\| \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}q\_1 - \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}q\_2\\|\_\infty < \\|q_1-q_2\\|\_\infty\\;.
$$


{{% toggle_block background-color="#FAD7A0" title="Proof" default-display="none"%}}
For any $q\_1, q\_2 \in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$ we have, thanks to (6):
$$
\begin{aligned}
     \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}q\_1 - \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}q\_2 &= q\_1 - q\_2 + (\mathbf{I}-\lambda\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\mu)^{-1}(\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi(q\_1-q\_2) - (q\_1-q\_2)) \\;,\\\
&= q\_1 - q\_2 - (\mathbf{I}-\lambda\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\mu)^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\lambda\mathbf{P}\_\pi)(q\_1-q\_2) \\;,\\\
&= (\mathbf{I}-\lambda\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\mu)^{-1}\big(\lambda\mathbf{P}\_\pi - \lambda\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\mu\big)(q\_1-q\_2)\\;. \\\
\end{aligned}
$$
By using the fact that $\mathbf{P}\_\mu$ is a stochastic matrix, we get that: 
$$
\begin{aligned}
     \\| \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}q\_1 - \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}q\_2\\|\_\infty &\leq \frac{1}{1-\gamma\lambda} \\| \lambda\mathbf{P}\_\pi - \lambda\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\mu\\|\_\infty \\| q\_1-q\_2\\|\_\infty  \\;, \\\
                &= \frac{\lambda}{1-\gamma\lambda} \\| \mathbf{P}\_\pi -\gamma\mathbf{P}\_\pi + \gamma\mathbf{P}\_\pi - \gamma\mathbf{P}\_\mu\\|\_\infty \\| q\_1-q\_2\\|\_\infty  \\;, \\\
                &\leq \frac{\lambda}{1-\gamma\lambda}\big( \gamma\\| \mathbf{P}\_\pi -\mathbf{P}\_\mu\\|\_\infty + 1-\gamma\big)\\| q\_1-q\_2\\|\_\infty  \\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
By definition of the sup-operator norm:
$$
\begin{aligned}
     \\| \mathbf{P}\_\pi -\mathbf{P}\_\mu\\|\_\infty &= \max\_{(s,a)} \sum\_{(s^\prime, a^\prime)} \left\vert [\mathbf{P}\_\pi]\_{(s,a),(s^\prime, a^\prime)} - [\mathbf{P}\_\mu]\_{(s,a),(s^\prime, a^\prime)}\right\vert\\;,\\\
            &= \max\_{(s,a)}  \sum\_{s^\prime} \sum\_{a^\prime} p(s^\prime\vert s, a)\big\vert\pi(a^\prime\vert s^\prime)-\mu(a^\prime\vert s^\prime)\big\vert\\;,\\\
            &= \max\_{(s,a)}  \sum\_{s^\prime} p(s^\prime\vert s, a)\\|\pi(s^\prime) - \mu(s^\prime)\\|\_1\\;,\\\
            &\leq \max\_{s^\prime}\\|\pi(s^\prime) - \mu(s^\prime)\\|\_1\\;,
\end{aligned}
$$
where we used the fact that $p(\cdot\vert s, a)$ is a probability distribution.
Together with the previous bound:
$$
\\| \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}q\_1 - \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}q\_2\\|\_\infty \leq \frac{\lambda}{1-\gamma\lambda}\big(\gamma\max\_{s}\\|\pi(s) - \mu(s)\\|\_1 + 1-\gamma\big)\\| q\_1-q\_2\\|\_\infty\\;.
$$
Combining with the assumption $\max\_s\\|\pi(s)-\mu(s)\\|\_1< (1-\lambda)/(\gamma\lambda)$ yields the announced result.
<div style="text-align: right"> $\blacksquare$&nbsp; </div>
{{% /toggle_block %}}

Altogether we get the guarantee that when the behavioural policy resembles the target policy, the operator $\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}$
and a fixed-point algorithm will lead us to the computation of its fixed point $q\_\lambda^\pi$. 
Regarding the stochastic approximation, let us denote
$\delta\_k^{\pi} = r\_k + \lambda\mathbb{E}\_{a\sim\pi(s\_{k+1})} q(s\_{k+1}, a) - q(s\_k, a\_k)$. The forward view writes:
$$
q(s\_k, a\_k) \leftarrow q(s\_k, a\_k) + \alpha \sum\_{t\geq k} \gamma^{t-k} \lambda^{t-k} \delta\_t^\pi \\;.
$$
This is an immediate consequence of (5).
Again, observe the resemblance with the on-policy SARSA($\lambda$) update—but with the trace generated by $\mu$ and the Bellman residual taken under $\pi$.


{{% toggle_block background-color="#CBE4FE" title="A multistep view of $Q(\lambda)$" default-display="none"%}}
The operator (5) is provided as-it, but it actually has a multistep origin. For any $n\geq 1$, define:
$$
\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, n}^{\pi, \mu} q(s, a) := r(s, a) + \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^\mu\big[\sum\_{t=1}^n \lambda^t\big(r\_t + \mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim\pi(s\_t)} q(s\_t, a^\prime) - q(s\_t, a\_t)\big) + \lambda^{n+1}\mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim\pi(s\_{n+1})} q(s\_{n+1}, a^\prime)\big]\\;.
$$
Each step carries a _reward correction_ that folds the target policy $\pi$ back in through the value estimate. Averaging exponentially over the horizon recovers (5):
$$
\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q := (1-\gamma)\sum\_{k\geq 1}\gamma^{k-1}\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, k}^{\pi, \mu}\\;.
$$
The derivation mirrors the telescoping argument used for (4); denote $\Delta\_n =\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, n}^{\pi, \mu} - \mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, n-1}^{\pi, \mu}$; then:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\_n q(s, a) &=
\mathbb{E}\_{s,a}^\mu\Big[\lambda^n\big(r\_n + \mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim\pi(s\_n)}q(s\_n, a^\prime)-q(s\_n, a\_n)\big) + \lambda^{n+1} \mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim\pi(s\_{n+1})}q(s\_{n+1}, a^\prime)-\lambda^{n} \mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim\pi(s\_{n})}q(s\_n, a^\prime)\Big]\\\
&= \mathbb{E}\_{s,a}^\mu\Big[\lambda^n r\_n - \lambda^n q(s\_n, a\_n) + \lambda^{n+1}\mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim\pi(s\_{n+1})}q(s\_{n+1}, a^\prime)\Big]\\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
Summing and telescoping (mirroring the second proof of (4)) gives a new expression for
$\mathcal{Q}\_{\lambda, n}^{\pi, \mu}$; plugging it in the multi-step averaging definition and re-arranging the sum yields (5).
{{% /toggle_block %}}



## Retrace {{< ref link="retrace">}} [4]{{< /ref>}}

The main issue with $Q(\lambda)$ is that it is _unsafe_: contraction is only guaranteed when $\pi$ and $\mu$ are close enough. Whenever the proximity condition is not checked, 
we lose convergence guarantees.
The Retrace operator aims at getting the best-of-both-worlds from the importance sampling and $Q(\lambda)$ approaches.
We introduce Retrace directly in the same exponential-average form as (5):
$$
\tag{7}
\mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q(s, a) := q(s, a) + \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\big[\sum\_{t\geq 0} \lambda^t \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)\big(r\_t + \lambda\mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim \pi(s\_{t+1})}[q(s\_{t+1}, a^\prime)] - q(s\_t, a\_t)\big)\big]\\;,
$$
where $c^\gamma\_t := \gamma \min(1, \pi(a\_t\vert s\_t)/\mu(a\_t\vert s\_t))$, and where by convention
the empty product equals 1 (for $t=0$).
Before interpreting the operator's definition, we start with a few key properties;
first, that it contracts wrt the supremum norm and _without_ any condition between $\pi$ and $\mu$. For any $q\_1, q\_2\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$:
$$
\\| \mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q_1 - \mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q_2 \\|\_\infty < \\| q\_1-q\_2\\|\_\infty\\;.
$$
Second, that $q\_\lambda^\pi$ is its (unique) fixed point—which clears the (stochastic) fixed-point algorithms' healthiness. 
{{% toggle_block background-color="#FAD7A0" title="Proof" default-display="none"%}}
The proof starts by a short rewrite of the operator; for any $q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q(s,a) &= \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\big[\sum\_{t\geq 0} \lambda^t \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)\big(r\_t + \lambda\mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim \pi(s\_{t+1})}[q(s\_{t+1}, a^\prime)]\big)\big] - \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\big[\sum\_{t\geq 1}\lambda^t  \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)q(s\_t, a\_t)\big]\\;,\\\
&= \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\big[\sum\_{t\geq 0} \lambda^t \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)\big(r\_t + \lambda\mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim \pi(s\_{t+1})}[q(s\_{t+1}, a^\prime)]\big)\big] - \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\big[\sum\_{t\geq 0}\lambda^{t+1}  \big(\prod\_{s=1}^{t+1} c^\gamma\_s\big)q(s\_{t+1}, a\_{t+1})\big]\\;,\\\
&= \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\big[\sum\_{t\geq 0}\lambda^t  \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)\big(r\_t + \lambda\mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim \pi(s\_{t+1})}[q(s\_{t+1}, a^\prime)] - \lambda c\_{t+1}^\gamma q(s\_{t+1}, a\_{t+1})\big)\big]\\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
As a result, and denoting $\Delta q = q\_1-q\_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q\_1(s,a) - \mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q\_2(s,a) &= \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\left[\sum\_{t\geq 0}\lambda^{t+1} \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)\big(\mathbb{E}\_{a^\prime\sim \pi(s\_{t+1})}[\Delta q(s\_{t+1}, a^\prime)] - c\_{t+1}^\gamma \Delta q(s\_{t+1}, a\_{t+1})\big)\right]\\;,\\\
&= \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\left[\sum\_{t\geq 0} \lambda^{t+1}\big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)\sum\_{a^\prime} \big(\pi(a^\prime\vert s\_{t+1}) - c\_{t+1}^\gamma \mu(a^\prime\vert s\_{t+1})\big) \Delta q(s\_{t+1}, a^\prime)\big)\right]\\;,
\end{aligned}
$$
where we slightly abuse notation on $c\_{t+1}^\gamma$ and invoke the tower rule to marginalize $a\_{t+1}$.
As a result, for any $q\_1, q\_2\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{A}}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\\| \mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q\_1(s,a) - \mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q\_2(s,a) \\|_\infty &\leq 
\mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\left[\sum\_{t\geq 0}\lambda^{t+1} \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)\sum\_{a^\prime} \big\vert\pi(a^\prime\vert s\_{t+1}) - c\_{t+1}^\gamma \mu(a^\prime\vert s\_{t+1})\big\vert\right] \\| q\_1 - q\_2\\|\_\infty\\;,\\\
&\overset{(i)}{=} \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\left[\sum\_{t\geq 0}\lambda^{t+1} \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s\big)\sum\_{a^\prime} \big(\pi(a^\prime\vert s\_{t+1}) - c\_{t+1}^\gamma \mu(a^\prime\vert s\_{t+1})\big)\right] \\| q\_1 - q\_2\\|\_\infty\\;,\\\
&= \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\left[\sum\_{t\geq 0}\lambda^{t+1} \big(\prod\_{s=1}^tc^\gamma\_s\big)\big(1-c\_{t+1}^\gamma\big)\right]  \\| q\_1 - q\_2\\|\_\infty\\;,
\end{aligned}
$$
where $(i)$ uses that $c\_t^\gamma < \pi(a\_t\vert s\_t) / \mu(a\_t\vert s\_t)$. 
Denoting $C := \sum\_{t\geq 0}\lambda^{t} \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\mu}\big[\prod\_{s=1}^tc^\gamma\_s\big]$ we get:
$$
\\|\mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q\_1(s,a) - \mathcal{R}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q\_2(s,a)\\|\_\infty \leq \big(1 - C(1-\lambda)\big)\\| q\_1-q\_2\\|\_\infty\\;.
$$
Since $C>0$ and $\lambda <1$, we have $1 - C(1-\lambda) <1$, which finishes the proof.

The fact that $q\_\lambda^\pi$ is a fixed-point is immediate by the tower-rule and the fact that
$\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^\pi q\_\lambda^\pi = q\_\lambda^\pi$. The uniqueness is a direct consequence
of the operator's contractive nature.
<div style="text-align: right"> $\blacksquare$&nbsp; </div>
{{% /toggle_block %}}

Observe the contrast between (5) and (7); the 'rectified' Bellman error at round $t$
is now multiplicatively weighted by $\prod\_{s=1}^t c^\gamma\_s$. 
This multiplicative correction matches the $\gamma^t$ factor of $Q(\lambda)$
whenever the importance ratios are not _clipped_ by the $\text{min}$ operator; otherwise, the importance ratio kicks in to softly 'cut' the trace.
That importance ratios are clipped is a classical way of reducing variance, by trading it off with bias.
The forward view of the associated stochastic approximation writes:
$$
q(s\_k, a\_k) \leftarrow q(s\_k, a\_k) + \alpha \sum\_{t\geq k} \lambda^{t-k}\big(\prod\_{u=k+1}^t c\_u^\gamma\big) \delta\_t^\pi\\;,
$$
with $\delta\_t^\pi$ the same Bellman residual as for $Q(\lambda)$. This is an immediate consequence of (7).


{{% toggle_block background-color="#CBE4FE" title="On-policy" default-display="none"%}}
Observe that in the on-policy case ($\pi=\mu$) both $Q(\lambda)$ and Retrace fall back to SARSA($\lambda$).
{{% /toggle_block %}}


## Qtrace {{< ref link="qtrace">}} [5]{{< /ref>}}

Both $Q(\lambda)$ and Retrace require sampling new actions from $\pi$—either to materialise 
$\mathbb{E}\_{a\sim\pi(s)}[q(s, a)]$ or an unbiased estimator thereof. 
To avoid this and rely only on the experience gathered by $\mu$, one can further rely
on importance sampling—with some clipping to control variance.
This gives rise to the Qtrace estimator:
$$
\mathcal{K}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu} q (s, a) := q(s, a) + \mathbb{E}\_{s, a}^{\pi, \mu} \big[\sum\_{t\geq 0} \lambda^t \big(\prod\_{s=1}^t c\_s^\gamma\big) \big(r\_t + \lambda \rho\_{t+1} q(s\_{t+1}, a\_{t+1}) - q(s\_t, a\_t) \big)\big]\\;,
$$
with $\rho\_t = \min(\rho, \pi(a\_{t}\vert s\_{t})/\mu(a\_{t}\vert s\_{t}))$ and  $\rho>1$.
Observe that for $\rho\to\infty$, we recover essentially the Retrace operator (the stochastic approximation will differ because of the importance ratio, though).
While this operator does have a fixed point, it is not the value function of an actual policy—and 
clearly not $q\_\lambda^\pi$. The authors of {{< ref link="qtrace">}} [5]{{< /ref>}} provide upper-bounds on 
the error between said fixed-point and the actual $q\_\lambda^\pi$ we are after. 


## Vtrace {{< ref link="vtrace">}} [6]{{< /ref>}}
Surely a better way to appreciate Qtrace is via its predecessor Vtrace—which is a state value function operator. 
Reusing notations, the operator writes for any $v\in\mathbb{R}^\mathrm{S}$:
$$
\mathcal{V}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}v(s) = v(s) + \mathbb{E}\_s^\mu\big[\sum\_{t\geq 0} \lambda^t \big(\prod\_{s=1}^{t-1} c\_s^\gamma\big)\rho\_t\big(r\_t + \lambda v(s\_{t+1}) - v(s\_t)\big) \big]\\;.
$$
Similar to Qtrace, its unique fixed-point is _not_ $v\_\lambda^\pi$—but the value function $v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}$ of the policy $\pi\_\rho$
that interpolates between $\mu$ and $\pi$; with $\eta(s)$ the suitable normalisation constant, for any $s, a\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$:
$$
\pi\_\rho(a\vert s) := \eta(s) \min(\rho \mu(a\vert s), \pi(a\vert s))\\;.
$$
For $\rho\to\infty$, we recover a state-value version of Retrace, and the fixed-point policy $\pi\_\rho$ collapses to $\pi$. 
At the other side of the spectrum, for $\rho\to 0$, $\pi\_\rho$ falls back to $\mu$ (to see this, materialise the 
normalising constant $\eta$).

{{% toggle_block background-color="#FAD7A0" title="Proof" default-display="none"%}}
We will not prove contraction, which requires only a small modification of the Retrace proof. 
To prove that $v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}$ is a fixed-point for the operator $\mathcal{V}\_{\lambda, \gamma}^{\pi, \mu}$ it suffices, 
thanks to the tower-rule, to prove that:
$$
\mathbb{E}^\mu\big[\rho\_t\big(r\_t + \lambda v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s\_{t+1}) - v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s\_t)\big)\big\vert s\_t\big] = 0\\;.
$$
By extending the l.h.s, for any $s\in\mathcal{S}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbb{E}^\mu\big[\ldots\big\vert s\_t=s\big] &= \sum\_{a} \mu(a\vert s) \min\Big(\rho, \frac{\pi(a\vert s)}{\mu(a\vert s)}\Big)\Big(r(s, a) + \sum\_{s^\prime}p(s^\prime\vert s, a)v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s^\prime)-v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s)\Big)\\;,\\\
&= \sum\_{a} \mu(a\vert s) \min\Big(\rho, \frac{\pi(a\vert s)}{\mu(a\vert s)}\Big)\Big(r(s, a) + \sum\_{s^\prime}p(s^\prime\vert s, a)v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s^\prime)\Big) - v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s) \\;, \\\
&= \sum\_{a}\min(\rho \mu(a\vert s), \pi(a\vert s))\Big(r(s, a) + \sum\_{s^\prime}p(s^\prime\vert s, a)v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s^\prime)\Big) - v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s) \\;, \\\
&\propto \mathcal{T}\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho} v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s) - v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}(s)\\;,\\\
&= 0\\;.
\end{aligned}
$$
since $v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}$ is a fixed-point of $\mathcal{T}\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}$.
<div style="text-align: right"> $\blacksquare$&nbsp; </div>
{{% /toggle_block %}}

Clearly, in the general case $\rho \in (0, +\infty)$ this falls short of our goal, which was to estimate 
$q\_\lambda^\pi$.
To understand the practical use of Vtrace, it needs to be replaced in a broader policy iteration context
(in the case of {{< ref link="vtrace">}} [6]{{< /ref>}}, the Impala algorithm). 
On top of the evaluation performed by Vtrace, a policy improvement step can prefer to improve over
the safer $\pi\_\rho$—slightly 'less off-policy' than $\pi$, wrt the behavioural $\mu$.


## Summary

The off-policy operators covered in this post can be compared along a
handful of axes: the trace factor $c\_t$, the fixed point they target, whether contraction holds unconditionally, and whether the stochastic
approximation requires sampling from $\pi$. We summarise the key differences in the table below.

<div style="display: flex; justify-content: center;">

| Operator     | Trace factor                      | Fixed point                 | Contracts                                                                   | Needs $\pi$ samples? |
|--------------|------------------------------------------|-----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------|
| $Q(\lambda)$ | $\gamma$                                 | $q\_\lambda^\pi$            | $\lVert \pi-\mu \rVert\_\infty < (1-\lambda)/(\lambda\gamma)$ | yes                  |
| Retrace      | $\gamma\cdot\min(1, \pi/\mu)$            | $q\_\lambda^\pi$            | yes                                                                         | yes                  |
| Qtrace       | $\gamma\cdot\min(1, \pi/\mu)$            | $\neq q\_\lambda^\pi$       | yes                                                                         | no                   |
| Vtrace       | $\gamma\cdot\min(1, \pi/\mu)$            | $v\_\lambda^{\pi\_\rho}$    | yes                                                                         | no                   |

</div>
